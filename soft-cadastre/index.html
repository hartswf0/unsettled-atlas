The Soft Cadastre

A working register for Atlanta, in two parts: what individuals say about places, and what institutions claim about them on the record. Everything here is held as bounded, uncertain ground. Nothing is sharpened into a boundary it did not earn.

Reading the register…

01What this is

A cadastre is the official register of land. It records who holds which parcel, in hard lines, with the state standing behind every boundary. It is the most confident document a city produces.

This is the other one. It registers what people say about places — which is almost never a parcel, and almost never a line.

Someone writes that the BeltLine is wonderful early but impossible to ride by brunch. Someone stands up at a neighbourhood meeting and says the traffic from that development will come down our street. Someone leaves a note on a map saying a park entrance no longer exists. A zoning board writes the word denied.

Each of those is a claim about territory, made in language. Language is bounded but not sharp: "behind Ponce City Market" is a real place with real edges that nobody could draw. So this register holds every such claim as a soft extent — a field with a shape and a width — and refuses, at every stage, to harden it into a line it did not earn.

Two registries answer to one contract. The first holds what individuals said, gathered from public map notes. The second holds what institutions claimed, compiled from neighbourhood planning minutes. Same instrument, pointed at the two ways a city talks about itself: one person at a time, and one meeting at a time.

    04Where the material comes from

    Registry I — acquisition

    OpenStreetMap Notes are the only source in the register that runs with no key, no agreement and no records request: a public API of people reporting that the map and the ground disagree. That disagreement is testimony about place, which is exactly what this registry holds.

    The ingester tiles the city's bounding box, because the API caps how much it returns per request, and it warns when a tile comes back full. A silent truncation would read on the map as a quiet neighbourhood. Note authors' usernames are deliberately not stored; the population is recorded, not the person.

      Registry II — the loop

      Every stage writes a line to a ledger beside itself. The ledger is not logging. It is the record of what the machine claims it did, kept next to the thing it made, so the two can be compared later by someone who was not there.

        05Gates
        
  
        Five, and they are executable. gate.mjs runs each one, prints what failed and which stage fixes it, and exits non-zero if any failed. A gate that only says false makes the next person guess.
        
  
        
    
    
        №The gate asksState
        
    
  
        
  
        Gate 2 is the one worth defending. It refuses to let you spend compute on a corpus that names a different place every time. If the top five anchors cover less than a third of the units, you do not have a world — you have a travelogue, and the fix is to grow the gazetteer, not the compute budget.
        
  
        Gate 5 is the one that cannot be satisfied by working harder. It requires a person to open the viewer and read every frame. Nothing in the pipeline can write that record for you, and the pipeline stays failed until someone does. A frame nobody read is a frame nobody checked.

                09What was tried and refused

                10What is wrong with it
                
  
                Stated plainly, because the alternative is a register that flatters itself.
                
  
                
    
                Both registries currently run on invented material. Registry I holds thirty authored claims. Registry II's minutes are written to exercise the pipeline and its anchor coordinates are eyeballed. Nothing here is a finding about Atlanta.
                
    
                An acquired corpus was built, published, and then withdrawn. See the section above. It was reported as a result before anyone read the text it contained.
                
    
                Registry I has no LOOK gate, and that is why. Registry II cannot pass until a human has read every frame. Registry I had no equivalent, so two hundred and twenty-eight unreviewed claims went straight into the atlas and onto both surfaces. The asymmetry is the bug; the bad corpus was only what it let through.
                
    
                The basemap can mislead. Drawing a road beneath a claim tempts a reader to place the claim on that road. It locates the reader, never the claim.
                
    
                The two registries do not share a place identifier. A claim from one and a territory box from the other can be about the same corner and the system cannot tell. This is the most interesting missing join, because it is where personal testimony and institutional record would be forced to disagree in public.

                  Keeping the copies honest
                  
  
                  Surfaces that read JSON over fetch cannot do so from a file path, so each ships a generated script twin. Two owners, one each, and both exit non-zero on drift:
                  
  
                  node sync-embeds.mjs --check                 # this page's registry twin
node seres/harness/build-atlas.mjs --check   # the atlas, its twin, and the helm
                  
  
                  A twin that drifts is a surface showing different numbers depending on how it was opened — exactly the kind of quiet lie this system exists to refuse.
